Made for India,
Except—

A Brand Audit

By Aadhya Gupta · BBA, IITM (GGSIP University) · Delhi-NCR · 2026

This is not a college assignment. There is no submission deadline, no professor grading this, no rubric to follow. This is a portfolio document, built for recruiters, marketing professionals, and anyone who wants to see how a marketing student thinks when the guardrails come off.

↓
The Hook

April 2026.
India is watching.

Screenshots are circulating. Not of a product failure, not of a data breach, of a document. An internal grooming guide, quietly circulated inside one of India's most visible consumer brands, containing a single line that would detonate across X, Instagram, and primetime news within hours:

"Bindi is not allowed."

The brand in question had spent fifteen years telling India it was built for India. It had 2,800+ stores across the country. A $5 billion valuation. Eight million paying members. A founder on Shark Tank who spoke, season after season, about democratising vision care for every Indian.

And somewhere inside that brand, someone had written that a bindi was unprofessional.

Within 48 hours, boycott calls were trending. Political figures were walking into stores on camera. The CEO was issuing clarifications on X. A Pongal ad from 2025 resurfaced, people started asking why the festive spread had dates on a banana leaf.

"Dates. On a Pongal plate."

Two incidents. One week. One question that neither a press statement nor a revised style guide could fully answer: "How does a brand that says 'Made for India' end up here?"

◆ Marketing Lesson

The moment a brand's external promise outpaces its internal systems, a crisis isn't a matter of if, it's a matter of when. Remember that as you read everything that follows.

Section 01

Who Is Lenskart?

Lenskart is an Indian omnichannel eyewear company launched in 2010 by Peyush Bansal, Amit Chaudhary, and Sumeet Kapahi. It began as an online platform when most Indians still bought glasses from neighbourhood opticians, and set out to rebuild the entire category from scratch.

In fifteen years, Lenskart grew into India's largest organised eyewear retailer, serving 40M+ customers across 175+ cities, 2,800+ stores globally, and a $5B valuation. FY24 revenue: ₹5,428 crore, up 43% YoY. EBITDA: ~₹856 crore.

The brand also made deliberate portfolio moves: John Jacobs (premium, design-forward sub-brand incubated within Lenskart) and Owndays (Japan-origin, majority stake acquired in 2022, ~$400M) anchoring international ambitions across 13 Asian markets.

Section 02

Brand Identity

A brand identity audit asks whether every visual and verbal signal a brand puts into the world says the same thing, consistently, across every touchpoint, not just whether a logo looks nice. For a brand running 2,800+ stores across 14 countries, that consistency is infrastructure.

The logo centres on a loop-like glasses icon that doubles as an infinity symbol, simple, scalable, instantly recognisable. Where it falls short is distinctiveness: competent, but not so unique it couldn't belong to another modern eyewear player.

The verbal identity faces a similar gap. "India's Biggest Eyewear Brand" is a claim of scale, not a promise of value. The gap between the banner-friendly market claim and the richer narrative of access, care, and innovation remains unresolved into a single, consistently expressed core message.

◆ Marketing Lesson

A strong visual system creates recognition. A strong cultural system creates belonging. Lenskart has the first. The second is what this audit interrogates.

Section 03

Brand Positioning

Positioning is not a tagline or a mood board. It is the answer to one brutally simple question: in the mind of your target customer, what do you own, and why should they choose you over everyone else?

Lenskart's most consistent narrative, across founder interviews, brand content, and investor material, is that eyewear in India was broken, expensive, and inaccessible, and Lenskart fixed it. The numbers lend this credibility: 40M+ customers, Tier-2/3 expansion, home eye-test service.

The "Made for India" positioning is one of the strongest claims any Indian consumer brand has made in the last decade. Geographic, cultural, and aspirational simultaneously. But it sets a higher bar: the brand has to live that identity not only in advertising but in how it treats employees and responds to cultural sensitivities.

The unorganised sector, thousands of neighbourhood opticians, still accounts for ~70–80% of India's eyewear market by value. When Lenskart calls itself "India's Biggest Eyewear Brand," it is the largest player in the organised segment, not the total market.

◆ Marketing Lesson

Positioning is a promise. The bigger the promise, the higher the standard of proof. "Made for India" is an extraordinary claim. The April 2026 crisis is what happens when the internal reality doesn't match the external promise.

Section 04

Campaign History

Lenskart has built its brand through a mix of Peyush Bansal's personal founder-brand (Shark Tank India made him the most visible face of Indian entrepreneurship), celebrity partnerships, and digital-first marketing. The results have been commercially effective, but culturally inconsistent.

2010–2015
Category Education Phase
Early campaigns focused on educating Indian consumers that glasses could be bought online, a genuine behavioural shift. Home eye-test, try-before-you-buy, free home delivery. The messaging was functional and trust-building.
2016–2019
Omnichannel Expansion, "See the World"
Rapid store rollout paired with digital campaigns around self-expression and style. Lenskart moved from "affordable glasses online" to "fashion-forward eyewear for everyone." Celebrity tie-ups began. John Jacobs launched as a premium sub-brand.
2020–2023
Shark Tank & Founder Brand Era
Peyush Bansal's Shark Tank India presence drove massive brand awareness, arguably Lenskart's most effective marketing in a decade. The founder became the brand. "Democratising vision care for every Indian" became the central narrative. Emotionally resonant, widely trusted.
2024–2025
Scale Phase. The Pongal Incident
A Pongal 2025 advertisement featuring what appeared to be dates on a banana leaf, a culturally inaccurate depiction of a Tamil festival. Nobody noticed at the time. The ad would resurface in April 2026 as evidence of a pattern, not an isolated mistake.
◆ Marketing Lesson

Cultural mistakes rarely stand alone. When one surfaces, audiences go looking for others. Lenskart's Pongal ad was forgotten until the bindi crisis gave people a reason to remember it.

Section 07 — The Crisis

When The Brand Breaks

April 2026
The Bindi Crisis

An internal grooming guide circulates on X and Instagram containing the line: "Bindi is not allowed." Screenshots are shared by employees, consumers, and public figures within hours.

Boycott calls trend within 24 hours. Political figures visit stores on camera. The CEO issues a clarification on X, but the statement is widely perceived as damage control, not accountability.

The Pongal ad resurfaces. Two incidents, one week, one conclusion in the public mind: "Made for India" is a marketing claim, not a cultural commitment.

◆ Why This Hit So Hard

The bindi is not a fashion accessory. It is a religious and cultural symbol worn by Hindu women. Banning it in a workplace is not a dress code decision, it is a statement about whose identity is considered professional. For a brand that has built 15 years of equity on "Made for India," the contradiction was impossible to spin away.

◆ The Structural Cause

This was not a rogue HR manager's mistake. This was a symptom of a brand that grew faster than its internal culture could keep up with. When a brand's external promise ("Made for India") outpaces its internal systems (HR policies, cultural training, regional sensitivity), the crisis isn't a question of if, it's a question of when.

◆ Marketing Lesson

Every brand eventually faces a moment where internal reality and external promise collide publicly. The brands that survive are the ones that had already done the internal work before the crisis arrived. Lenskart had not.

Section 08 — Crisis Strategy

The Playbook

What should Lenskart actually do, not just in the immediate days after the crisis, but in the 90-day window that determines whether it recovers or stagnates? This is not a PR brief. This is a brand strategy recommendation.

Phase 1 — Days 1–7: Own It, Don't Manage It
Authentic acknowledgement over damage control
The CEO's X statement needs to go further than clarification. It needs to name the failure, not "this policy has been revised" but "we failed to uphold the India we claim to be built for." Generic crisis communication will not work for a brand that has made cultural identity its core promise.
Phase 2 — Days 7–30: Internal Action, External Proof
Show the work, not just the statement
Publish a concrete action plan: cultural sensitivity training (who, when, verified by whom), revised HR policy (shared publicly), an internal cultural advisory board with diverse employee representation. Actions that are verifiable, not promises that are forgettable.
Phase 3 — Days 30–90: Rebuild the Promise
Earn "Made for India" again, from the inside out
Launch a campaign that doesn't talk about the crisis, but demonstrates what "Made for India" actually means in practice. Not a hero film. A series of store-level stories: the employee in Chennai who designed frames for traditional silk sarees. The optometrist in Lucknow who learned to conduct eye tests in three regional dialects. Real humans, real India, no filters.
The Long Game
Culture is not a campaign, it is the product
The deepest recommendation: Lenskart needs to build internal cultural intelligence as a business function, not an HR afterthought. A brand with 2,800+ stores across 175 cities and 14 countries, in a country as regionally and culturally diverse as India, cannot operate on a single uniform cultural standard. Diversity is not a PR message. It is an operational requirement at this scale.
◆ Final Marketing Lesson

The strongest brands are not the ones that never face a crisis. They are the ones whose internal culture is so coherent with their external promise that when a crisis comes, the recovery is already built in. That is the standard Lenskart set for itself. That is the standard this audit holds it to.

"The gap between what a brand says and what it does is always, eventually, measured by its customers."
